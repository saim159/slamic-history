01 · Introduction

Who was Al-Ghazali?

Abu Hamid Muhammad ibn Muhammad al-Ghazali (d. 1111 CE) was a major Muslim intellectual whose writings engaged with Islamic law, theology, philosophy, ethics and spiritual practice.

His work cannot be reduced to a single discipline. He studied philosophical arguments, participated in theological debates, wrote on religious practice and explored the relationship between knowledge, intention and human conduct. His intellectual legacy remains the subject of both admiration and scholarly debate. [1]

Historical date note: Al-Ghazali's death is generally dated to 1111 CE. His birth year is disputed: traditional accounts often give 1058–1059, while Frank Griffel argues for approximately 1055–1056. This page therefore does not present one birth year as certain. [1]
02 · Origins

Early life and historical setting

Al-Ghazali was born in the region of Tus in Khurasan, in the eastern part of the medieval Islamic world. The area formed part of a wider intellectual landscape shaped by scholarly networks, religious institutions and the political power of the Seljuk dynasty. [1]

His life unfolded during a period in which legal scholarship, theology, philosophy and Sufi practice interacted in complex ways. These traditions were not isolated worlds: scholars could study, use and criticise ideas across disciplinary boundaries.

Understanding this setting helps explain why al-Ghazali's writings address questions of religious authority, the limits of knowledge, moral responsibility and the purpose of learning.

03 · Intellectual formation

Education and teachers

Al-Ghazali received early education in his native region and later studied in Nishapur under the prominent scholar Imam al-Haramayn al-Juwayni. This association was important to his development as a scholar of law and theology. [1]

His intellectual formation included engagement with argumentation, logic and theological questions. His later writings show familiarity with philosophical positions as well as the methods used to examine them.

Context matters: Al-Ghazali's later criticism of particular philosophers should not be confused with a lack of knowledge of philosophy. His critiques developed through engagement with the arguments he examined.

05 · Career and interpretation

Baghdad and the departure of 1095

Al-Ghazali's position in Baghdad brought him into a prominent intellectual and political environment. His career was connected with the Seljuk regime, and his departure in 1095 has become one of the most discussed episodes in accounts of his life.

In Al-Munqidh min al-Dalal, often translated as Deliverance from Error, al-Ghazali describes his search for certainty and concerns about the relationship between knowledge and sincere action.

Modern historians have questioned whether this autobiographical narrative alone fully explains his departure. Kenneth Garden examines the political context of the Seljuk regime and argues that al-Ghazali's intellectual concerns before 1095 were more continuous with his later work than a simple story of sudden transformation might suggest. [2]

Interpretive caution: The spiritual explanation is important because al-Ghazali himself presents it in his writings. It should nevertheless be read alongside historical context and modern scholarship, rather than treated as the only possible explanation.
06 · Selected writings

Major works

The following works illustrate the range of al-Ghazali's interests. The summaries are introductory; they do not replace reading the texts themselves or consulting specialist scholarship.

Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din

Commonly translated as The Revival of the Religious Sciences, this extensive work discusses worship, ethical conduct, the inner dimensions of religious practice and the development of character.

Ethics · Worship · Spiritual life

Tahafut al-Falasifa

Known as The Incoherence of the Philosophers, this work examines and criticises particular philosophical positions, especially on metaphysical questions.

Philosophy · Metaphysics · Theology

Al-Munqidh min al-Dalal

Often translated as Deliverance from Error, this autobiographical and intellectual account discusses the search for certainty and engagement with different paths of knowledge.

Knowledge · Autobiography · Certainty

Mishkat al-Anwar

Commonly rendered as The Niche of Lights, this work explores the symbolism of light in relation to Qur'anic language and spiritual knowledge.

Qur'anic symbolism · Spiritual knowledge

These works belong to different genres and address different audiences. They should not be treated as if they all make the same argument or were written for the same purpose.

07 · Intellectual debates

Al-Ghazali and philosophy

Al-Ghazali's relationship with philosophy was complex. He examined philosophical arguments and criticised particular conclusions, but his engagement with philosophy also influenced the language and methods of later theological discussions. [1]

Tahafut al-Falasifa is particularly associated with his criticism of positions advanced by philosophers such as Ibn Sina and al-Farabi. The text addresses specific metaphysical claims rather than providing a blanket rejection of every form of reasoning or scientific inquiry.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy places al-Ghazali within the broader history of philosophical ideas entering Muslim theology. Its account helps complicate the popular claim that his work simply ended philosophy or science in the Islamic world. [1]

A useful distinction: Criticising an argument is not the same as rejecting every discipline associated with it. Historical claims about philosophy and science require evidence beyond the work of one thinker.
08 · Moral and spiritual thought

Knowledge, ethics and spiritual life

A recurring concern in al-Ghazali's writings is the relationship between what a person knows and how that person lives. Religious knowledge is discussed alongside intention, action, character and the condition of the heart.

In Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din, questions of worship and law are considered together with ethical and spiritual concerns. Kenneth Garden's study also emphasises the work's relationship to Islamic law, Sufism and philosophical ideas, challenging overly narrow descriptions of its intellectual background. [2]

This approach helps explain why al-Ghazali's writings have been studied by readers interested not only in abstract theology but also in moral psychology, education and everyday religious practice.

09 · Historical significance

Influence and legacy

Al-Ghazali became an influential figure in subsequent Islamic intellectual history. His work contributed to debates about reason, revelation, theology, philosophical method, religious authority and the ethical purposes of knowledge. [1]

His legacy is not interpreted identically by every scholar. Some accounts emphasise his spiritual transformation, while other studies stress the continuity of his intellectual projects, his political context and the rhetorical purposes of his autobiographical writing. [2]

A balanced assessment therefore considers his own texts, the historical circumstances in which he wrote, and the later traditions that interpreted his work.
